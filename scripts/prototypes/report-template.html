Does Vegas disagree with the model?

Polymarket runs a season-long "who leads fantasy points at each position" market for 2026–27. This page turns that into a dollar comparison against JP $, our bottoms-up model — but only for the handful of players per position anyone has actually traded, and using math that doesn't overstate what a betting favorite implies about point production. Rebuilt version — the first pass had two real bugs; see the callout below.

What changed from the first version: it redistributed each position's dollar pool in direct proportion to raw win-probability, which implied things like "Vegas expects Gibbs to outscore McCaffrey 4-to-1" — nonsense; our own projections have them 17% apart. Win-probability in a big field is a logarithmic function of the underlying stat line, not a linear one (think horse racing: a modest real speed edge can still make a horse a heavy favorite, because only the fastest one on the day wins). This version inverts that properly, and it only prices players with a real, currently-quoted bid — an untraded contract isn't "5% conviction," it's no information at all, and got assigned a market figure anyway last time (a backup, Isiah Pacheco, priced above Jonathan Taylor). Full explanation in Method & caveats below.

How much would a real points edge move the odds?

That's the one real assumption this whole page rests on, and it's genuinely uncertain, so it's a dial instead of a fixed number. A tighter assumption (left) says even small point gaps swing win-probability a lot, so a big betting favorite implies only a small real edge. A looser assumption (right) says it takes a bigger points edge to move the odds this much. Drag it — every number below updates live.

Method & caveats

The idea, in plain terms

A "who finishes #1" market doesn't hand out probability in proportion to how good a player is — it hands out probability in proportion to how likely he is to be the single best out of the whole field that day. Those aren't the same thing. A modest real edge can still make someone a heavy favorite, the same way a runner who's only slightly faster than the field can still be a 4-to-1 favorite to win outright, because only the fastest finish matters, not how everyone else compares to each other.

So instead of reading "32% vs. 6.85%" as "4.7x the points," we ask the reverse question: how big a real points edge would it take to produce a 32%-vs-6.85% favorite? That's the β slider above — it's the assumed spread (standard deviation) of season-total outcomes. The math: points_i − points_anchor = β × ln(P_i / P_anchor), converted to dollars at our model's own rate of $__STARTER_RATE_DISPLAY__/point. Every table anchors to that position's own market favorite (Gibbs, Nacua, McBride, Allen) at his existing JP $, and computes everyone else relative to him.

Where this still breaks down

  • β isn't measured, it's assumed — that's the whole reason it's a slider and not a fixed number. Treat any single reading as illustrative, not precise.
  • "Real" means bestBid ≥ 2¢ — still thin by sportsbook standards, just no longer indistinguishable from an untraded stub.
  • These are "leads the league" markets, not yardage/points over-unders, and still carry the injury/role risk of a full season for one player.
  • JP $ here uses generic 12-team/$200/default-roster settings, not this league's actual saved settings.
